\begin{abstract}
We improve both ends of the standard tables of bounds on $K_q(n,R)$, the
minimum cardinality of a $q$-ary code of length $n$ with covering radius $R$,
in the range $5\le q\le 15$. On the upper-bound side we give eighteen improved
bounds, obtained by an engineered focused local search seeded with structural
constructions; to our knowledge these are the first improvements to any upper
bound on $K_q(n,R)$ with $q\ge 5$ since the 2011 freeze of K\'eri's tables,
and they include reductions of up to $23\%$
($\Kq{6}{8}{4}\le 166$) below bounds that had stood for decades. On the
lower-bound side we give seven improved bounds for $6\le q\le 10$, obtained
from an exact-arithmetic reimplementation of the Gijswijt--Polak semidefinite
programming hierarchy, whose published results cover only $q\le 5$; every new
lower bound is backed by a rational dual certificate that a standalone,
dependency-free checker verifies in exact arithmetic, so the bounds do not
rest on floating-point computation. One cell is improved from both sides:
$441\le \Kq{6}{10}{4}\le 2784$, against the previous $417$--$2952$. Beyond
the bounds themselves we report the algorithm engineering that produced them
--- a shared-sphere incremental evaluation that speeds up the local search
$6$--$10\times$, structural solvers over cosets of linear codes and
translation quotients, a measured portfolio policy, and a controlled
comparison of CPU and GPU implementations --- together with the exhaustive,
redundant verification protocol. All eighteen codes and all seven
certificates are provided in machine-readable form with standalone verifiers.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Let $\Zq^n$ denote the Hamming space of $q$-ary words of length $n$. A code
$C\subseteq \Zq^n$ has \emph{covering radius} $R$ if every word of $\Zq^n$ is
within Hamming distance $R$ of some codeword. The function
\[
K_q(n,R) \;=\; \min\{\,|C| : C\subseteq \Zq^n \text{ has covering radius} \le R\,\}
\]
is a central quantity of covering-code theory~\cite{CHLL}; the binary case
$K_2(n,1)$ includes the domination number of the hypercube, and the ternary
case $K_3(n,1)$ is the classical football pool problem.

Upper bounds on $K_q(n,R)$ are established by explicit constructions, lower
bounds by combinatorial or convex-relaxation arguments, and the state of the
art is recorded in the tables of K\'eri~\cite{Keri}, which subsume and extend
the earlier tables of Cohen, Honkala, Litsyn and Lobstein~\cite{CHLL}. The
last update to K\'eri's tables was made in November 2011. Recent activity has
concentrated on lower bounds for small alphabets: Wu and Chen~\cite{WuChen}
improved binary lower bounds via congruence arguments, and Gijswijt and
Polak~\cite{GijswijtPolak} obtained many new lower bounds for $q\le 5$ from a
semidefinite programming hierarchy. For alphabets $q\ge 5$ we are not aware
of any published improvement on either side since the 2011 freeze; a
systematic search of the literature (arXiv, DBLP, OpenAlex, Lobstein's
covering-code bibliography, and the publication lists of the authors active
in this area) found none.

This paper improves both sides in that range: eighteen upper bounds for
$5\le q\le 15$ (Table~\ref{tab:ub}) and seven lower bounds for $6\le q\le 10$
(Table~\ref{tab:lb}).

Two structural observations explain where the improvements come from. On the
upper-bound side, K\'eri's source annotations show that for moderate $q$ and
$R\ge 3$ most tabulated bounds are \emph{inherited arithmetic} --- direct sums
\begin{equation}\label{eq:directsum}
K_q(n_1+n_2,\,R_1+R_2)\;\le\;K_q(n_1,R_1)\cdot K_q(n_2,R_2),
\end{equation}
the special case $K_q(n+1,R)\le q\,K_q(n,R)$, the monotonicity
$K_q(n+1,R+1)\le K_q(n,R)$, and product rules over alphabet factorizations
--- cells in which no search was ever run. Those cells fall furthest (up to
$23\%$). But the engineered search of Section~\ref{sec:method} also shaves
several cells whose incumbents came from dedicated search or codes in the
2000s (the cells with keys \texttt{n}, \texttt{q}, \texttt{z} in
Table~\ref{tab:ub}), by $1$--$6\%$. On the lower-bound side, the size of the
symmetry-reduced Gijswijt--Polak SDP depends only on $n$ and not on $q$, so
their $q\le 5$ boundary was one of compute and numerical precision rather
than of model size; carrying the same relaxation into $6\le q\le 10$ with
exact certification yields improvements precisely on the cells whose
incumbent lower bounds are weak relative to the sphere-covering bound
(Section~\ref{sec:lb}).

The results were produced autonomously by an AI system on a single
GH200 node over roughly one week; we describe the methodology --- including a
frozen-rules competition between independently developed solvers, used to
select the production algorithm --- in Sections~\ref{sec:method}
and~\ref{sec:engine}, and the verification protocol, which we regard as the
load-bearing part of any record claim, in Section~\ref{sec:verify}.

\section{Upper bounds: the search}\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Focused local search}\label{sec:search}

We search for a code of prescribed size $M$ minimizing the number of
uncovered words. The state is a multiset of $M$ codewords together with the
coverage count $\mathrm{cnt}(w)$ of every $w\in\Zq^n$; a move changes one
coordinate of one codeword. The move update is cheap because of the
following observation.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:move}
Let $c'$ be obtained from $c$ by setting coordinate $p$ to $v\ne c_p$. Then
the set of words leaving the radius-$R$ ball of $c$ and the set of words
entering the ball of $c'$ are both indexed by the words at distance exactly
$R$ from $c$ in the coordinates other than $p$; consequently a move updates
exactly $2S$ coverage counters with
$S=\binom{n-1}{R}(q-1)^R$, the two sets are disjoint, and distinct counter
updates address distinct words.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Since $d(w,c') = d(w,c) + [\,w_p=c_p\,] - [\,w_p=v\,]$, a word can leave the
ball of $c$ only if $d(w,c)=R$ and $w_p=c_p$, and can enter the ball of $c'$
only if $d(w,c')=R$ and $w_p=v$; writing such a word as its restriction to
the coordinates $\ne p$ (at distance exactly $R$ from the restriction of $c$)
plus its value at $p$ gives the stated bijections, disjointness
($w_p=c_p$ versus $w_p=v$), and injectivity.
\end{proof}

The search itself is a focused local search in the WalkSAT lineage, close in
spirit to the tabu searches that produced much of the original
tables~\cite{Ostergard,vanLaarhoven}: pick a random uncovered word $u$;
collect as candidate moves every codeword at distance exactly $R+1$ from $u$
nudged one coordinate towards $u$ (if no codeword is that close, the
codewords at minimum distance from $u$ are used); evaluate all candidates
exactly and in parallel; commit a best candidate, ties broken uniformly at
random. On stagnation a codeword is teleported onto an uncovered word.
Parameter studies on $\Kq{6}{6}{3}$ showed that \emph{candidate breadth
dominates}: evaluating all candidate moves exactly outperformed sampling 24
or 64 of them (final uncovered counts $0$ versus $82$ and $18$), while tabu
tenures and WalkSAT-style noise were harmful on these instances. Simulated
annealing sustained a $33\times$ higher move rate and still lost to the
focused search --- move quality beats move count here.

For each cell we first rebuilt a code of the incumbent size from the same
construction that produced the incumbent bound (e.g., for $\Kq{6}{10}{4}$ the
direct sum \eqref{eq:directsum} of an optimal $\Kq{6}{4}{1}$ code of size 72
and a $\Kq{6}{6}{3}$ code of size 41, both found by direct search and
verified). Descent then proceeds by remove-and-repair: delete the codeword
whose private coverage (words covered by no other codeword) is smallest, and
repair the deficit by local search; on success, repeat at $M-1$. Searching
the small factors and re-composing (a ``factor attack'') was decisive for
seeding; the improvements themselves come from the descent breaking out of
the product structure.

\subsection{Algorithm engineering}\label{sec:engineering}

The search described above spends essentially all of its time in one place, and
saying exactly where turns out to determine what is worth optimising.

\subsubsection*{Profile}

Instrumenting each phase of the solver with \texttt{CLOCK\_THREAD\_CPUTIME\_ID}
on three representative cells --- $\Kq{6}{6}{3}$ at $M=41$ ($q^n=46\,656$),
$\Kq{6}{8}{4}$ at $M=169$ ($q^n=1\,679\,616$) and $\Kq{8}{9}{4}$ at $M=2944$
($q^n=134\,217\,728$) --- gives candidate evaluation $92.9\%$, $98.5\%$ and
$99.6\%$ of the run time respectively. Committing a move takes $3.3\%$,
$0.8\%$ and $0.4\%$; choosing the uncovered word, computing the $M$ Hamming
distances and breaking ties together account for under $2\%$ everywhere.
The cost of one evaluated sphere pattern grows from $1.9$\,ns to $6.0$\,ns to
$7.4$\,ns as the counter array grows from $93$\,kB (L2-resident) to $3.4$\,MB
(L3) to $268$\,MB (DRAM): the walk is a strided gather and its throughput is
set by the working set, so reducing \emph{bytes touched} pays as well as
reducing \emph{patterns walked}.

\subsubsection*{Sharing one sphere among the moves of a codeword}

Lemma~\ref{lem:move} localises both the leaving and the entering set of a move,
and a closer reading shows that the leaving set depends on the moved
coordinate only through a membership predicate. Write $D(w)=\{j: w_j\neq c_j\}$
and let $c^{(p)}$ denote $c$ with coordinate $p$ replaced by $v$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:shared}
$w$ leaves the ball of $c$ under the move at $p$ if and only if $|D(w)|=R$ and
$p\notin D(w)$; and $w$ enters the ball of $c^{(p)}$ if and only if
$|D(w)|=R+1$, $p\in D(w)$ and $w_p=v$.
\end{lemma}

Consequently, putting
\[
T=\#\{w: |D(w)|=R,\ \mathrm{cnt}(w)=1\},\qquad
H_j=\#\{w: |D(w)|=R,\ \mathrm{cnt}(w)=1,\ j\in D(w)\},
\]
the number of words that the move at $p$ newly uncovers equals $T-H_p$ for
\emph{every} $p$ at once. A single enumeration of the full distance-$R$
sphere, of size $\binom{n}{R}(q-1)^R$, therefore replaces the $R+1$ separate
enumerations of $\binom{n-1}{R}(q-1)^R$ patterns each, a saving of
$(R+1)(n-R)/n$ (2.00, 2.50 and 2.78 on the three cells). The updates to $T$
and $H$ are executed only when $\mathrm{cnt}(w)=1$, which on the loose cells
this method targets happens for $0.4\%$ of words at $M=169$ and $0.07\%$ at
$M=216$ in $\Kq{6}{8}{4}$, so the inner loop reduces to one load and one
compare --- half the memory traffic per pattern of the original.

\subsubsection*{The entering side from the uncovered words}

The entering side does not share in the same way: enumerating the
distance-$(R+1)$ sphere once costs $(q-1)n/(R+1)^2$ times the per-position
walks, which is worse for these parameters. But the entering side counts only
words with $\mathrm{cnt}(w)=0$, and by Lemma~\ref{lem:shared} membership of
the entering set is decided in $O(n)$ from the digits of the word. Maintaining
the set $U$ of uncovered words explicitly therefore replaces
$(R+1)\binom{n-1}{R}(q-1)^R$ counter reads by $O(n|U|)$ work, which is
profitable exactly when
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ulcond}
n\,|U| \;<\; (R+1)\binom{n-1}{R}(q-1)^R .
\end{equation}
The right-hand side is $5\,000$, $109\,375$ and $840\,350$ on the three cells
while the mean $|U|$ during a run is $58$, $30$ and ${\approx}10^4$, so
\eqref{eq:ulcond} holds by two to four orders of magnitude for the whole
useful life of the search. The solver tests \eqref{eq:ulcond} each iteration
and falls back to the sphere walk when it fails, so it is never worse.
$U$ is kept as an append-ordered array of (index, digit vector) pairs together
with a presence bitset over $\Zq^n$; commits append the words they uncover,
stale entries are removed by an amortised compaction, and no $q^n$-sized index
array is needed.

Together, the counter reads per candidate codeword fall from
$2(R+1)\binom{n-1}{R}(q-1)^R$ to $\binom{n}{R}(q-1)^R$, a factor of $4.00$,
$5.00$ and $5.56$.

\subsubsection*{A wider neighbourhood for free}

$T-H_p$ does not depend on the value $v$ written into coordinate $p$, and the
entering counts read off $U$ are naturally indexed by the pair $(p,v)$ at no
extra cost. One sphere enumeration plus one pass over $U$ therefore yields the
exact objective change of \emph{all} $n(q-1)$ single-coordinate moves of a
codeword, not only the $R+1$ that move it towards $u$. Since the parameter
study of Section~\ref{sec:search} found candidate breadth to be the dominant
tuning knob, widening the neighbourhood from $R+1$ to $n(q-1)$ moves per
codeword --- a factor of $7.5$ to $11$ here --- is the natural way to spend
the saving.

\subsubsection*{Narrower counters on the read path}

Coverage counters are $16$-bit because $M$ may exceed $255$, but evaluation
only ever asks whether a counter is $0$ or $1$. A side array
$\mathrm{st}(w)=\min(\mathrm{cnt}(w),2)$ carries everything the read path needs
in one byte or in two bits, while the exact counters stay for the write path;
the extra write costs nothing measurable because commits are under $1\%$ of the
time. On $\Kq{8}{9}{4}$ the two-bit form shrinks the read path from $268$\,MB
to $33.5$\,MB, bringing it inside the $117$\,MB last-level cache.

\subsubsection*{Correctness}

Every one of these changes is an implementation change: it computes the same
objective differences as the reference solver and must therefore follow the
same search trajectory from the same seed. That is verified bit-for-bit over
24 configurations ($q$ from 2 to 7, $R=0$, $R=n-1$, sizes above and below the
optimum) $\times$ 9 variants, and separately the number of uncovered words the
solver reports is required to equal the number the standalone verifier
computes from the written code file --- including the runs that end at a
nonzero count.

\subsubsection*{What did not help}

Several plausible ideas were implemented and measured, and did not work; we
record them because the reasons are structural rather than accidental.

\emph{Caching move evaluations between iterations} is impossible here. A
cached value for codeword $c$ survives a commit on $c'$ only if their
evaluation spheres are disjoint, which needs $d(c,c')>2R+2$; but $2R+2\ge n$
for every cell of interest, so every commit invalidates every entry. Large
$R$ relative to $n$ --- the defining feature of the cells whose bounds are
inherited arithmetic --- is exactly what rules caching out.

\emph{Early exit on a lower bound.} During a candidate's walk the final
change is bounded below using the fact that the entering count cannot exceed
$|U|$. The bound is exact and prunes $1.03\%$, $0.006\%$ and $0.000\%$ of
patterns on the three cells: it only becomes strong as $|U|$ falls, and small
$|U|$ is precisely what makes the sparse evaluator fast instead.

\emph{Inverting the leaving side}, i.e.\ maintaining a list of the singly
covered words as we do for the uncovered ones, pays only if
$n|U_1| < \binom{n}{R}(q-1)^R$. Measured on the recorded codes, $|U_1|$ is
5357, 6592 and ${\sim}10^6$ against thresholds of 417, 5469 and 33\,614: it
fails everywhere. Uncovered words are rare by construction, singly covered
words are not.

\emph{Symmetry reduction.} Translating the code so that one codeword sits at
$0^n$ and freezing it quotients out the $q^n$ translations, but gives no
measurable benefit ($4.87\times$ against $5.26\times$ on $\Kq{6}{8}{4}$,
$0.89\times$ against $2.11\times$ on $\Kq{6}{6}{3}$). A local search does not
revisit states exactly, so removing a symmetry removes almost no work; all it
does here is shrink the candidate pool by one codeword, which on the small
cell measurably hurts.

\emph{Deterministic focus on the longest-uncovered word}, the cheapest form
of a guided objective, is clearly harmful: $2.04\times$ against $5.26\times$
on $\Kq{6}{8}{4}$, and on $\Kq{6}{6}{3}$ only 5 of 20 runs reached the target
at all. Always choosing the same word destroys the randomisation that lets
focused local search leave a plateau. A stochastic age weighting remains
untested.

\emph{Software prefetching} and \emph{transparent huge pages} are both within
noise. For huge pages the reason is architectural: the base page on this host
is already 64\,kB, so even the 268\,MB counter array spans only 4096 pages.

\subsubsection*{Restart policy}

The empirical run-time distributions answer the restart question directly. At
targets the search reaches routinely the distribution is light-tailed
(coefficient of variation $0.20$--$0.47$ on the two loose cells) and a fixed
restart interval cannot help; calibrating the best observed gain against the
memoryless null gives $p=1.0$. On the tight cell $\Kq{6}{6}{3}$ at a target
hard enough that a quarter of runs stall, the distribution becomes heavier
than exponential and restarting every ${\approx}1.6$ CPU seconds cuts the
expected time to target by a factor of $4.2$ ($p=0.002$). The policy that
follows is to run one chain while it is still improving and to rely on
independent parallel workers rather than on timed restarts.

\section{Upper bounds: structure, and the production portfolio}\label{sec:engine}

\subsection{Structural solvers}\label{sec:structure}

Free search over subsets of $\Zq^n$ is the wrong tool for cells whose optima
are algebraic, and several of K\'eri's incumbents are exactly that. Two
structural solvers close the gap.

\emph{Unions of cosets of a linear code.} When $q$ is a prime power and
$M = c\,q^k$, we search for codes of the form
$C=\bigcup_{i=1}^{c}(x_i + L)$ with $L$ a linear $[n,k]_q$ code. Covering is
then a property of syndromes: $C$ covers $\Zq^n$ iff the $c$ syndromes of the
$x_i$ cover the $q^{n-k}$-element syndrome space under the weighted distance
induced by coset-leader weights, collapsing a $q^n$-word problem to a
$q^{n-k}$-syndrome one. The effect is drastic on the cells it fits: the
incumbent $\Kq{8}{8}{4}\le 512=8^3$ --- which free search could not even
reproduce (its best from scratch was $740$) --- is reconstructed and verified
in $0.18$ seconds including process start-up, and the descent to the new
record $505$ then starts from a verified incumbent. The same solver settles
$\Kq{5}{11}{5}\le 625=5^4$ and the known-hard $\Kq{3}{11}{4}\le 81=3^4$ (a
cell on which the free search plateaued $410$ words short) in under a second
each.

\emph{Translation quotients.} A second solver searches codes invariant under
$x\mapsto x+\mathbf{1}$, working in the quotient (size $q^{n-1}$ effective)
with a genuinely free remainder: measurements on $\Kq{6}{8}{4}$ showed that
neither a fully invariant code (which provably plateaus, since one spare
codeword cannot repair a deficient orbit) nor a fully free search solves the
cell, while ``mostly invariant plus a free remainder'' solves it reliably.
On the largest cells the quotient search beats the best free search by
$17\%$ at equal core-seconds.

\subsection{A solver competition with frozen rules}\label{sec:arena}

The production configuration was selected by a competition rather than by
intuition. Four solver lineages --- the algorithm-engineering line of
Section~\ref{sec:engineering}, a low-level SIMD line, the structural line of
Section~\ref{sec:structure}, and a portfolio-scheduling line --- were
developed independently against a published contract: a fixed entry-point
interface, $\le 6$ threads, $\le 25$\,GB, frozen scoring (a full verified
cover scores $1000$; a partial cover scores the negated uncovered fraction;
invalid output scores worse than any valid partial), public development
cells, and \emph{held-out} judging cells revealed only at evaluation time,
15 seeds each, all entries sharing one machine in one window. The judge
verifies every output file independently; no solver's self-reported count is
ever trusted. One scoring amendment was made before judging (normalising
partial scores by $q^n$) after a competitor demonstrated that the original
absolute penalty let invalid output outscore valid partial covers on large
cells.

The outcome was not a single winner but a decomposition: the structural line
won the held-out judging outright (11/28 solves), the engineering line had
the fastest evaluator, the low-level line had the fastest inner loop on
cache-resident cells and the only deadline-safe initialisation and
atomic-publishing discipline, and the portfolio line's scheduling (many
single-threaded chains, stop on first solve) beat multi-threading everywhere
except the largest cells. The production engine is the merge of all four,
each component verbatim where it won:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Phase A (algebra, seconds):} the coset solver over every
feasible $k$; seeds from recorded codes and from direct sums over every
split, taken from the growing result set.
\item \textbf{Phase B (portfolio):} independent single-threaded chains with
distinct engines, seeds, and symmetry settings; the mix decided by measured
thresholds ($q^n$, redundancy $q^n/(M|B_R|)$, initialisation cost against
budget). Below $3\cdot 10^5$ words half the chains run the SIMD engine;
above $5\cdot 10^7$ the portfolio is all-quotient; the wide neighbourhood is
enabled near redundancy $\ge 3$; timed restarts only on tight cells.
\item \textbf{Discipline:} the wall clock covers initialisation; the
incumbent is published atomically on every improvement, so a kill at any
moment leaves the best code on disk; a finishing pass re-reads the winner
from disk, de-duplicates, and recomputes coverage by direct ball marking.
\end{itemize}

Under the frozen judge the merged engine was at least as good as every
component on every cell and strictly better on three of four public cells
(14/20 solves against the best component's 12/20, and 2.2--2.9$\times$
faster to a cover where both solve). All record cells were then attacked
with the merged engine under a descend-until-wall protocol.

\subsection{Hardware, and a controlled CPU/GPU comparison}\label{sec:gpu}

All eighteen record codes were found on the 64 Neoverse-V2 cores of a single
NVIDIA GH200 node; per-cell effort ranged from seconds to a few core-days.
Because the promise of GPU acceleration for this problem family is a natural
question, we report a controlled comparison on the same node (a 480\,GB
GH200, H100-class GPU).

Two GPU formulations were implemented and verified. The first casts
candidate evaluation as small integer matrix products over coverage
bitmaps to engage the tensor cores; it is structurally starved, because the
contracted dimension is only $nq\approx 50$ ($30\times$ below the
arithmetic-intensity knee of the hardware), and never approached the CPU.
The second runs 256 independent search chains in one persistent kernel
(one chain per thread block, no host synchronisation, the whole counter
state resident in HBM). At equal validated search semantics, the persistent
kernel reaches $0.09\times$ the throughput of the full 64-core socket on
$\Kq{6}{6}{3}$, $0.10\times$ on $\Kq{6}{8}{4}$, and $1.24\times$ on the
largest benchmarked cell $\Kq{8}{9}{4}$ ($q^n=1.3\cdot 10^8$, aggregate
$1492$ versus $1200$ moves/s) --- the regime where each chain's working set
defeats the CPU caches while the GPU's bandwidth advantage applies. The
honest summary is that on this problem a GPU currently buys about one extra
CPU socket of capacity on the very largest cells and is counterproductive
below $q^n\sim 10^8$; all bounds reported here are CPU results.

\section{Lower bounds: exact certificates for $q\ge 6$}\label{sec:lb}

\subsection{The relaxation}

Gijswijt and Polak~\cite{GijswijtPolak} bound $K_q(n,R)^3$ below by a
semidefinite program over variables indexed by orbits of triples of words
under $\mathrm{Aut}(q,n)=S_q\wr S_n$, with positive-semidefiniteness
constraints from the block-diagonalisation of the Terwilliger algebra of the
Hamming scheme, linear constraints from sphere-covering inequalities, and
matrix-cut constraints (their Theorem 4.18). Two facts make the $q\ge 6$
corner attackable. First, the size of the reduced SDP depends only on $n$
--- at $n=11$ it has 339 variables and 126 blocks of size at most 12 ---
so alphabet size costs nothing; $q$ enters only through the coefficients.
Second, published work stops at $q\le 5$, so the incumbents at $q\ge 6$ are
the 2011 table values.

We reimplemented the reduced SDP from scratch with the entire model
generation in exact integer arithmetic, and validated the implementation
four ways: (a) a self-test that pushes explicit verified covering codes
through the model and checks every constraint in exact rational arithmetic
(objective exactly $|C|^3$); (b) a coefficient-level comparison against the
authors' published Julia code, matching every generated coefficient ---
orbit partitions, objective, block-diagonalisation, distribution numbers ---
over six $(q,n,R)$ cells spanning $q=3$ to $7$, tens of thousands of
big-integer values each; (c) reproduction of their published values: over
the 49 published table values we recomputed, 44 match to published display
precision, and 18 of the 22 published \emph{improved lower bounds} we
attempted are reproduced exactly at the integer level (the misses are all in
precision-limited territory, below); (d) the invariant that no certified
lower bound may exceed a known upper bound, checked against all 1145 table
cells --- no violation ever occurred.

\subsection{Certification: nothing floating-point is trusted}\label{sec:lb:cert}

The SDP is solved numerically (an interior-point method; a first-order
solver as fallback), but the solver's output is used only as a
\emph{suggestion}. By weak duality, any feasible point of the dual ---
multipliers $y_k\ge 0$ for the linear constraints and PSD matrices $Y_b$ for
the blocks --- certifies the bound it attains, regardless of how it was
found. We therefore round the numerical dual to rationals over a dyadic
denominator ($2^{46}$), push each $Y_b$ strictly into the PSD cone, and, if
any dual inequality $d_v\le c_v$ fails in exact arithmetic, shrink the whole
dual by a dyadic factor $\theta$, which restores exact feasibility at the
cost of scaling the bound. A well-conditioned solve gives $\theta=1$.

The resulting certificate --- the rationals $y_k$, $Y_b$ and the claimed
value --- is checked by a standalone program of about seven hundred lines
that uses only the Python standard library: it \emph{re-derives the entire
SDP} from $(q,n,R)$ in exact integer arithmetic, checks $y_k\ge 0$, checks
each $Y_b\succeq 0$ by an exact symmetric-pivot $LDL^{\mathsf T}$
factorisation over rationals, checks every dual inequality in integer
arithmetic, and takes the cube root by exact integer comparison. It also
checks that the covering inequality supplied in the certificate is itself
valid, so a certificate cannot smuggle in an unsound constraint; tampered
certificates are rejected. The seven bounds of Table~\ref{tab:lb} are
precisely the cells (out of 163 certified) where the certified value exceeds
the 2011 incumbent. Every number in Table~\ref{tab:lb} rests on this checker
alone; the floating-point solve is not part of the trusted base.

\subsection{Conditioning, and where the improvements land}\label{sec:lb:where}

Making the interior-point method survive these instances was the main
technical obstacle: the optimum is $|C|^3\sim 10^{13}$ while every
constraint constant is $O(1)$, and the objective coefficients span ten
orders of magnitude, so a naive scaling collapses on exactly the interesting
cells (the solver reports spurious infeasibility, or the rounded dual loses
half its value to $\theta$). The scaling that works is a geometric-mean
equilibration, applied entirely in powers of two so that the map back to the
unscaled dual is exact: normalise the objective by the cube of the
sphere-covering bound and give each variable the magnitude profile of the
square root of its objective contribution. On $\Kq{6}{8}{3}$ this lifts the
certified value from $183$ to $240$ against a true SDP optimum of $239.52$;
on $\Kq{6}{10}{4}$ from $56$ to $441$.

Even so, double precision carries the method only to SDP values around
$1.5\cdot 10^9$ (cube root ${\approx}1150$); beyond that the solver fails in
a way the pipeline detects automatically (the SDP provably dominates the
sphere-covering bound, so any certificate below it flags a failed solve
rather than a weak bound --- such cells are reported as failures, never as
bounds). This ceiling, not the model, is what limits the present sweep: 68
of the 163 certified cells are in the failed-solve state, concentrated on
the large-$R=2$ cells where the incumbents are barely above sphere-covering
and the head-room is greatest, and several failures are near-misses
($\Kq{20}{8}{4}$ certified $2615.7$ against an incumbent $2760$;
$\Kq{21}{8}{4}$ $3135.1$ against $3288$). A higher-precision solver
(the published $q\le 5$ results used 512-bit arithmetic) would open that
range; we did not have one for this hardware.

The pattern of the seven successes is exactly what this analysis predicts:
every improved cell has an incumbent within $1$--$4\%$ of the
sphere-covering bound (K\'eri keys \texttt{y} and \texttt{s}), while no
cell with a Haas--Halupczok--Schlage-Puchta incumbent (key \texttt{m},
$13$--$52\%$ above sphere-covering) is improved --- at $q\ge 6$ this level
of the SDP hierarchy delivers $1.15$--$1.43\times$ sphere-covering,
decaying with $q$, and so overtakes the combinatorial bounds only where
they are weak. We also note that the fractional-covering \emph{linear}
program cannot help at all here: since $\mathrm{Aut}(q,n)$ acts
transitively on $\Zq^n$, averaging shows its optimum is exactly $q^n/|B_R|$
--- the LP \emph{is} the sphere-covering bound, which is why the track goes
straight to the SDP.

\section{Verification}\label{sec:verify}

False records are worse than no records, so verification is exhaustive,
exact, and redundant, and no solver's internal state is ever the evidence.

\emph{Upper bounds.} Every claimed code, re-read from its published file,
was checked by four methods, two of which share no code with the other two:
(1) ball marking: mark all words within distance $R$ of each codeword in a
$q^n$ bitmap and confirm the bitmap is full; (2) meet-in-the-middle bitset
covering over a coordinate split; (3) a min-distance scan over all $q^n$
words, which also reports the exact covering radius; (4) Hamming-graph
dilation: $R$-fold neighbourhood dilation of the code's indicator array,
computing no distances at all, with cost independent of $M$. In addition, a
fifth, independently written dilation verifier (produced without access to
the other implementations) re-confirmed all eighteen codes from the
published files immediately before this version was prepared. The largest
check ($q^n=6^{10}=60\,466\,176$ words) completes in seconds. The
distinct-codeword count of every file is checked against the claimed $M$.

\emph{Lower bounds.} Section~\ref{sec:lb:cert}: exact rational dual
certificates, an exact standalone checker, and the global invariant against
known upper bounds. The seven record certificates were re-verified
immediately before this version was prepared.

The ancillary files include \texttt{verify\_cov.py}, a standalone
dependency-free verifier (\texttt{python3 verify\_cov.py CODEFILE q n R}
re-checks any claimed upper bound in at most a few minutes; code files use
digits \texttt{0}--\texttt{9} then \texttt{a}--\texttt{z} for $q>10$), and
\texttt{certify.py} together with the seven certificates
(\texttt{python3 certify.py CERT.json} re-checks a lower bound, typically in
minutes).

\section{Discussion}

The pattern in the upper-bound experiments is clean: local search
consistently beat bounds that were inherited from general constructions
(relative slack between the previous upper bound and the sphere-covering
bound of $4\times$--$10\times$), shaved single-digit percentages off several
cells previously set by dedicated search, and consistently failed on cells
that were already the object of intensive search or algebraic construction
(slack $\le 3\times$), such as $\Kq{3}{11}{4}\le 81$ and $\Kq{6}{6}{3}\le
41$. K\'eri's tables contain further cells of the first kind for
$q\le 21$; the three highest-redundancy cells we attacked were still
improving at every budget we gave them, and we expect the marked bounds in
Table~\ref{tab:ub} to keep falling.

On the lower-bound side the boundary is equally clean, and it is a precision
boundary, not a model boundary: the same relaxation that produced the seven
new bounds certifies values within a few percent of the incumbents on cells
an order of magnitude larger, and fails there only for lack of a
higher-precision interior-point solver on this hardware. That, and the
van Wee-type strengthenings of the linear side that the published hierarchy
uses at $q=2$ but no one has run at $q\ge 6$, are the obvious next steps.

Since the tables have had no maintainer since 2011, we also publish a
merged, machine-readable bounds table (K\'eri 2011, the post-2011
lower-bound literature, and the present improvements) alongside the code.
